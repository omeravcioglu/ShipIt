# 09 — Marketing, Audience & Virality

Games don't sell themselves. Marketing turns time and money into **audience**. Audience turns into **wishlists and sales**. The best audience is the one you **own** and bring from game to game.

## Decisions
- **Where does the founder's marketing time go?** Channels, content types, cadence.
- **Owned or rented audience?** Mailing list and community versus algorithm-dependent followers.
- **Which creators to court,** with free keys, paid sponsorships, exclusives or long-term programs?
- **Organic or paid?** High-variance free reach versus predictable paid reach.
- **How much hype?** Hype drives wishlists, and it also raises the bar reviewers judge you against.
- **Which promises to make?** Promises boost you now and bind you later.

## 1. The audience model

### Owned versus rented audience

| Owned (persists across games, converts best) | Rented (cheaper to grow, depends on algorithms) |
|---|---|
| Mailing list subscribers | Chirp, Clipz and ViewTube followers |
| Chorus members | Creator audiences |
| Vapor studio followers (notified of every release) | Press readers |
| Patronly supporters | Ad audiences |

Platform events can suddenly shrink rented reach ("Chirp deprioritizes posts with links"). Owned audience is harder to build and never disappears overnight. Converting rented followers into owned ones (newsletter sign-ups, Chorus invites, studio follows) is a recurring strategic goal.

### Fans have genre affinity

Every fan carries a **genre/tag affinity profile** built from the games they followed, bought and enjoyed. When you announce a new game, a fan's interest depends on how close it is to that profile.
- Make three roguelikes and your fans are roguelike fans. **A cozy farming game brings along only part of them.**
- At high **brand awareness** (14), the studio name itself begins to carry across genres. That's one of the late-game payoffs of reputation.

### The funnel

```
Reach → Engagement → Follow / Store visit → Wishlist → Purchase → Review & word of mouth → (more Reach)
```

**Audience stats tracked (from the brief, plus a few):** Chorus members, Vapor followers, wishlists per game, email subscribers, **Community Trust**, **Community Activity**, social followers per platform, and fan-base size and affinity.

## 2. Social platforms

| Platform | Reach model | Best content | Audience skew | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chirp** | Followers + reposts | GIFs, dev updates, memes, screenshots | Developers, press, core gamers | Weekly **#ShowcaseSaturday** window. Occasional "links deprioritized" events. |
| **Clipz** | **Algorithmic** (followers barely matter) | 15–60 second clips with a visual hook | Young, casual, cozy | Heaviest tail: most posts get 200–2,000 views, rare ones 1M+. Low conversion per view, huge volume. |
| **ViewTube** | Subscribers + recommendations and search | Devlogs, trailers, long-form | Core gamers | Slow to build. **Evergreen long tail.** Builds trust and the founder's personal brand. |
| **Threadit** | Communities + upvotes | Genre-community posts, AMAs | Core, hardcore | **Community standing:** participating builds it, spamming gets you banned from a community. High variance. |
| **LiveWire** | Live streaming | Dev streams | Community | Small reach, deep engagement |
| **Chorus** | Owned server | Updates, playtests, events | Your fans | Retention, feedback, free QA (§9) |
| **Mailing list** | Owned | Announcements, launches | Your most engaged fans | Open rate 25–40%, click rate 3–8%. The best conversion of any channel. |

## 4. How a post performs

```
Reach = base_reach(platform, followers)
      × Quality          (effort level × Marketing skill × the game's visual appeal / style "pop")
      × Hook clarity     (from the idea and execution — 04)
      × Platform fit     (content type × platform, 0.3–1.5)
      × Timing           (posting windows — hidden until Algorithm Sense; trend alignment; season)
      × Fatigue          (×0.9 per post beyond ~4/week on one platform; reposts ×0.5)
      × Algorithm state  (platform events)
      × Luck             (lognormal, σ ≈ 1.1: median ×1, about 1 post in 100 hits ×20 or more)
```

- **Conversions:** engagement 1–6%; follows 0.5–3% of reach (higher for small accounts); store clicks 0.5–3% if a store page exists; wishlists = clicks × store conversion (10).
- **Consistency bonus:** posting on a regular cadence improves follower retention and algorithm favor (+10–20%).
- **The real constraint is time.** A solo founder can usually spare 2–4 marketing blocks a week. Every one of them is a block not spent on the game or a gig. The simulator's baseline is about 6 wishlists per marketing hour once a store page exists (13).

## 5. Hook clarity and press story angles

- **Hook clarity** (0–10) multiplies every marketing conversion. It comes from the idea (04), from execution (does the first screenshot *show* the hook?) and from the *Hook Writer* perk.
- **Press covers stories, not games.** Each game and studio has potential **story angles**, each with a newsworthiness value:
  - *"Solo developer spent 4 years on this"*, *"A game about grief"*.
  - The founder's background (02): *"ex-AAA veteran goes indie"*.
  - *"Viral moment"*, *"record-breaking Fundstarter"*, *"award winner"*.
  - *"Accessibility pioneer"*, *"technical feat"*, *"local culture"*, *"controversy"*.
- Angles **expire** with the news cycle. Pitching the right angle to the right outlet is a skill (the *Press Savvy* perk).

## 6. Press

- About 15 generated **outlets**, each with reach, genre interests, personality (11), preferred angles and a relationship with you. **Journalists** are contacts (14).
- **Press kit** (1–2 blocks): screenshots, trailer, fact sheet.
- **Embargoes and review keys:** sending keys late means fewer reviews on launch day.
- **PR agency** ($5–15k/month): reaches more outlets, finds better angles, helps with crises.
- Press coverage converts to wishlists and sales directly, and it also **attracts creators** (press → creators → players).

## 7. Paid ads

| Detail | Value |
|---|---|
| Platforms | Chirp, Clipz, ViewTube pre-roll, Threadit, gaming ad networks |
| Price | CPM $4–15 (**+40% in Q4**); CPC $0.40–2.00 |
| Typical cost per wishlist | $1–5, depending on the creative, targeting, genre and store page |
| Creative fatigue | The same creative loses efficiency (×0.85 per week) |

- **Attribution is hidden by default.** Without analytics and tracking links, ad-driven wishlists show up as "Unknown source", so the player can't see ROI. The *Data Marketer* perk and analytics research reveal it (17).
- **The decision:** ads rarely pay for themselves on small premium games. With a strong trailer and store page, at scale, they can. Their value is **predictability**: low variance, unlike organic reach.

## 8. Influencers and streamers

**Creator attributes:**

| Attribute | Visibility |
|---|---|
| Platform, audience size, content style (let's play, review, speedrun, variety/chaos, cozy) | V |
| Engagement rate | E |
| Sponsorship price | V |
| **Genre preferences** | H: revealed by *Influencer Database* research or CreatorConnect filters |
| **Conversion rate** | H: learned from your campaign data |
| Integrity (does their audience trust sponsored content?) | E |
| Availability (booked months ahead) | V |
| **Relationship with your studio** | V: grows when they enjoy your games |
| Bug sensitivity (drops games that crash on stream) | H |

**Creator tiers** (the brief's examples are included):

| Tier | Subscribers | Sponsored price | Engagement | Base chance a free key gets covered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Micro | 1k–20k | Free keys / $50–300 | Very high (8–15%) | 15–30% |
| Small | 20k–100k | $300–1,500 (**40k subscribers ≈ $500**) | High | 8–15% |
| Mid | 100k–1M | $1.5k–15k | Medium-high | 3–8% |
| Large | 1M–5M | $15k–70k (**5M ≈ $70,000**) | Medium | 0.5–3% |
| Mega | 5M+ | $70k–250k+ | Medium | < 0.5% |

**Actions:** send free keys (a CreatorConnect campaign: volume versus targeting), a paid sponsored video, a sponsored stream, **exclusive early access** (creators love exclusives; comes with an embargo), creator playtests (feedback + relationship), and a **creator program** (long-term: revenue-share codes, early builds).

**Coverage and conversion:**

```
P(cover | key) = tier_base × genre_match (0.2–2.0) × streamer_appeal (0.5–2.0)
               × relationship (1–2) × exclusivity (1.5 if exclusive) × buzz (1–3) ÷ inbox saturation (busy seasons)
Views           = subscribers × view rate (5–20%) × content fit
Wishlists/sales = views × 0.5–3%   (enthusiastic organic coverage ×1.5; sponsored ×0.7 unless the creator's integrity is high)
```

**Risks:**
- A buggy build on a big stream: the crash clip spreads (negative virality) and the relationship suffers.
- The creator's own controversies.
- **Fake-engagement creators** (hidden): low conversion, revealed by campaign data.

**Organic discovery:** creators can find your game on their own (more likely with high buzz, store visibility and genre fit). That's where "Major YouTuber plays your game" comes from.

## 9. Virality: the buzz cascade

Virality is modeled as **coverage causing more coverage**, not a lottery ticket.

- **Buzz** per game is visible as a meter with a momentum arrow. It grows with coverage events (creator videos, breakout posts, press, festival features) and decays 10–15% a week.
- Each week, every creator has a chance to cover the game:
  `p_i = base_i × genre_match × streamer_appeal × (1 + k × Buzz) × relationship`
- The expected number of new coverage events triggered by one event is the **branching factor R**.
  - **R < 1:** coverage fizzles.
  - **R > 1:** a cascade. The game goes viral until it saturates the reachable audience for its genre.
- **Virality traits** come from features and ideas and raise streamer appeal and base chances:
  - *Funny Physics*, *Multiplayer Chaos*, *Reaction Moments* (horror), *Streamer Integration*, *UGC / Sandbox*.
  - *Clip-able Moments*, *Meme-able* (the pettable dog, absurd premises), *Speedrun-able*, *Short Session*.
- **Visible:** "Streamer appeal: Very High" and "Buzz: Rising".
  **Hidden:** R and the per-creator probabilities.
- **Virality never guarantees success.** Buzz only converts through the store page, price, quality and stability. A viral game with low quality produces refunds and negative reviews at scale ("viral, but bad").
- **Negative virality** uses the same cascade with a negative sign: crash compilations, asset-flip exposés, controversies.

## 10. Community

- **Chorus server** stats: members, activity (daily active %), sentiment (mood), moderation load (hours per week), superfans (count), toxicity.
- **Community Trust** (studio-wide, 0–100):
  - **Up:** transparency (devlogs), kept promises, acting on feedback, good updates, fair pricing, honest marketing.
  - **Down:** broken promises, silent delays, abandoned games, predatory monetization, misleading marketing, ignored bugs, controversies.
  - **Effects:** slightly kinder reviews, better crowdfunding, Early Access and Patronly conversion, resilience to review bombs, a word-of-mouth multiplier, and better fan conversion on wishlists.
- **Community Activity** (0–100): decays unless fed with updates, events and developer presence. High activity produces UGC (fan art, clips, mods), word of mouth, playtest volunteers and **free bug reports**.
- **Workload:** about 1 hour a week per 1,000 members for moderation and replies. Unmanaged communities trigger toxicity events and lose trust. A Community Manager handles about 20k members. **Superfans can become moderators** (free labor, with drama risk).
- **Feedback loop:** feature requests with vote counts, bug reports and complaints arrive through the community.
  - Acting on popular requests → trust +.
  - Ignoring them → slight trust −.
  - Saying "no" *with a reason* → almost no loss. Communication matters more than compliance.
- **Community events:** toxicity outbreak, a tester leaks the build, a fan-art contest takes off, a superfan becomes a moderator, moderator drama, "the community demands a feature you can't build", a fan wiki appears, a speedrun community forms, a modding scene emerges.

## 11. The Promises ledger

Every **public commitment** is recorded: release dates, roadmap items, stretch goals, Patronly rewards, "multiplayer coming soon", "no microtransactions, ever", "Nimbus version later", "a year of free updates".

| Stage | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Making a promise** | Immediate hype, wishlists and trust. That's why you make them. |
| **Keeping it** | Trust +, progress toward the "Delivers on promises" tag |
| **Breaking it** | Trust −, scaled by how many people saw it and how late it is. **Communicating early, with a reason, halves the damage.** Repeated breaks earn the "Broken promises" tag (14). |

Promises also **reduce flexibility**: cutting a promised feature breaks a promise (05). The UI shows the ledger like a debt schedule, because **promises are a loan from your community.**

## 12. Marketing plan, beats and hype

- **Beats:** announcement, trailer reveal, store page live, demo, festival, release-date announcement, launch, updates, awards.
- **Hype** (per game): each beat adds hype, which decays ~8% a week. Wishlist velocity follows hype. **Beats spaced 4–8 weeks apart** keep momentum; clustered beats waste each other.
- **Hype raises expectations.** Peak hype raises the expected-quality bar used in the review formula (05). If the game falls short of the bar, reviews say "overhyped", and refunds rise. The *Hype Machine* perk amplifies both sides.
- The **Marketing Timeline** shows beats on the calendar with predicted wishlist curves drawn as bands.

## 13. Delegation at scale

- A **Marketing Manager** executes a plan with budget and strategy settings (organic versus paid, platform mix, influencer spend) and sends a weekly report. The player chooses the beats and the big bets.
- A **Community Manager** runs Chorus and Threadit presence and moderation.
- Founder marketing blocks become strategic actions (a keynote at a showcase, an AMA), not daily posting.
